In most small businesses, the IT department is whoever is closest to the printer when it jams. That works right up until the day it does not: the server dies, the email stops, or someone clicks the wrong link.

You do not need a full-time IT person. But there are a handful of things every business needs to have covered. Here they are, in plain English.

## 1. Backups you have actually tested

Ask yourself: if the main computer in your office died tonight, what would you lose? Customer records? Years of invoices? Your accounting file?

Hard drives fail, laptops get stolen, and buildings flood. A proper backup is:

- **Automatic** — it does not depend on someone remembering
- **Off-site** — a copy lives somewhere other than your building
- **Tested** — someone has actually restored a file from it

A backup that has never been tested is a hope, not a backup.

## 2. Protection from scam emails and ransomware

Most attacks on small businesses start with an email: a fake invoice, a "password expired" notice, a message that looks like it came from the owner. One click can lock every file you have.

Small businesses are targeted precisely because criminals assume nobody is guarding the door. The basics go a long way:

- Up-to-date security software on every computer
- Software updates installed promptly, not postponed for months
- Two-step login on email and banking
- A five-minute talk with your staff about what a scam email looks like

## 3. Passwords that are not on a sticky note

The same password on every account, written on a note under the keyboard, shared by six people — it is more common than anyone admits. A password manager fixes this in an afternoon and makes life easier for everybody.

Just as important: when an employee leaves, their access should be turned off that day.

## 4. Wi-Fi and internet that just work

Slow, flaky internet costs you real money in wasted time, dropped card payments and frustrated customers. The fix is often simple: better equipment, placed correctly, with guest Wi-Fi kept separate from your business network.

## 5. Professional email

An email address at your own business name looks more established than a free account, and it keeps company email under the company's control instead of on someone's personal login. It is inexpensive and worth setting up properly.

## 6. Somebody to call

This is the one that matters most. When something breaks, you need a real person who knows your setup and can fix it quickly, often remotely within minutes, without you waiting on hold with a call center.

## What you probably do not need

- **A full-time IT employee.** Most small offices do not have enough work for one
- **The most expensive equipment.** Reliable and properly set up beats top-of-the-line
- **A long, complicated contract.** You should understand exactly what you are paying for

## One-time fixes or ongoing support?

There are two ways to handle IT:

- **Call when it breaks.** You pay only when there is a problem. Simple, but problems tend to arrive at the worst time, and nobody is watching for trouble in between
- **Ongoing support.** Someone keeps an eye on your systems, handles updates and backups, and catches issues early. It costs a predictable amount and prevents most emergencies

Which one fits depends on how much a day of downtime would cost you. For most businesses with more than a few computers, prevention is cheaper than recovery.

## A quick self-check

- Do you know when your files were last backed up?
- Could you get back to work if your main computer died today?
- Does every account have its own password?
- Do you know who to call when something breaks?

If you answered "no" or "not sure" to any of those, it is worth a conversation.
